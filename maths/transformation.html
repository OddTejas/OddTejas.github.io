How mathematicians cheat:

Changing perspectives

Problems involving the translation of a quadratic function usually begin the same way: find the vertex, convert the equation into vertex form, and only then move it horizontally or vertically.

Consider the following:

Translate the graph of the function $y=2x^2+3x+1$ by two units in the $x$-direction and by $-3$ units in the $y$-direction.

Solving this requires finding its vertex, either through calculus or algebraically by completing the square, and then transforming the vertex by the given values. Either way, we need to write it in vertex form to perform such operations.

This vertex form resembles the following: $$a(x-h)^2+k$$ where $(h,k)$ represents the coordinates of the vertex.

Is there any better way?

Converting it like this is a hassle, and we have better things to do. When I was solving this, I got the answer, but I was not satisfied because the process felt clumsy. When I was learning maths in intermediate, I was taught about the transformation of axes: changing the origin of a graph.
I didn't think much of it, but I remembered seeing a method of substituting values in an equation to transform its vertex. This looked like a transformation of axes, but why would something like this work? We are moving the graph and not the curve, and yet it works!

Now think: if you were looking at an object from the front and needed to see its other sides, you could rotate the object or change your position so that another side became visible. Either way, the shape of the object would not change—only your perspective would. Thus, what I thought was a clever “trick” was actually a clever implementation of the transformation of axes. One can simply transform the perspective instead of doing all the algebraic gymnastics.

If you notice, the distance from the vertex of the curve to the new origin remains the same no matter how much you change it. Moreover, the new axes remain the same distance from the curve as the old axes.

With this technique in mind, we can entirely bypass finding the vertex of the curve.

Proofs

The formula for the transformation of axes is the following: $$x = X-h$$ $$y = Y-k$$ where $x$ and $y$ are coordinates on the original axes, and $X$ and $Y$ are the new coordinates. If the original curve is $y=f(x)$, then the transformed equation is simply: $$y-k = f(x-h)$$ $$y=f(x-h)+k$$

Solving the original question

Given: $h=2$ and $k=-3$
$f(x)=2x^2+3x+1$
Substituting $h=2$ and $k=-3$:
$$2(x-2)^2 +3(x-2)+1-3 $$ By simplification, we arrive at: $$\boxed{2x^2 -5x}$$ Thus, mathematicians cheat by changing perspectives and finding ways to make problems easier to solve.
