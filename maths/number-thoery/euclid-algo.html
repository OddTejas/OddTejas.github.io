An Inconvenient Problem

Suppose we were given an ordinary GCD problem:

Question Statement

Find the greatest common factor between 119 and 221

The common method of finding factors does not seem to work well for numbers like 119 and 221 where there are no easily recognizable factors.

gcd⁡(221,119)=?\gcd(221,119) = ? Let us reframe the question with some simpler numbers: 21 and 14. Factoring tells us the gcd⁡(21,14)=7\gcd(21,14) = 7

Now, observe what we are about to do. First subtract the two number. 21−14=721 - 14 = 7 Interesting, the new number we get is also divisible by the GCD of the two numbers. Does this hold true for multiple numbers, lets check.

Numbers GCD Difference
(30,5)(30,5) 55 2525
(100,10)(100,10) 1010 9090
(16,4)(16,4) 44 1212

Yes! All of following are also divisible by their GCD.

Hidden relationship

From our trials we can see how there is a relationship between the difference and GCD of two numbers. Then does this suggest that can convert this bigger problem into a simpler one while preserving the common factors, think about that for a minute, two pairs of numbers, one simpler and one larger and both having the same answer. We will experiment about this.

By this logic we can rewrite,

gcd⁡(221,119)=gcd⁡(119,102)\gcd(221,119) = \gcd(119,102) which is not that much easier to the original question, but can we subtract these two number again? Yes, we can and doing so we get =gcd⁡(102,17)=17\begin{aligned} & = \gcd(102,17) \\ & = 17 \end{aligned} Doing a quick check we see 102=6*176 \ast 17 we see 17 as the highest common factor and as we have noted above how there exists a relationship we can also say, gcd⁡(221,119)=gcd⁡(119,102)=gcd⁡(102,17)=17\begin{aligned} & \gcd(221,119) \\ & = \gcd(119,102) \\ & = \gcd(102,17) \\ & = 17 \end{aligned}

From our little example we can come to the conclusion that GCD of two numbers is equal to GCD of the difference and any of the two numbers gcd⁡(30,5)=gcd⁡(25,5)=5\gcd(30,5) = \gcd(25,5) = 5

But why?

Can we prove that this method will work for any pair of positive integers?

For that we need to define what a common factor is. A common factor is a number that divides both number without a remainder i.e the remainder must be 0. Suppose there are two numbers adad and cdcd such that dd is the highest common factor and a,ca,c are coprime gcd⁡(ad,cd)=d\gcd(ad,cd) = d Now lets subtract the two numbers, assuming a≥ca \geq c. ad−cd=d(a−c)ad - cd = d(a - c) Aha! Subtracting preserved the common factor. lets us also add ad+cd=d(c+a)ad + cd = d(c + a) which also preserved the common factor, but we have to confirm that this process did not create a new common factor. Lets check for that too.

Suppose when we subtracted the numbers it brought some new factors then, lets add them back, as we have noted how addition preserved the existing factors, any new new factor introduced by subtraction must be present even when we add them. Doing so we get only d as the highest common factor, as we can see below. d(a−c)+cd=add(a - c) + cd = ad

However, this means that the supposedly new common factor divides both adad and cdcd, the two numbers we started with. So was it ever a new common factor?

Every common factor that exists after subtraction was there from the very beginning and every original common factor is preserved by subtraction.Then we can say, no common factors are created or lost, and the greatest common factor remains unchanged.

Thus gcd⁡(ad,cd)=gcd⁡(d(a−c),cd)=d\begin{aligned} & \gcd(ad,cd) \\ & = \gcd(d(a - c),cd) \\ & = d \end{aligned} where d is the highest common factor. We can also Add, for any two numbers gcd⁡(a,b)=gcd⁡(a+b,b)\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(a + b,b) but we don’t usually use this as it makes the numbers larger which is of no use when we want to simplify the expression

Should you stop?

When should we stop the process? You can repeat this process till the GCD can be easily recognized or when one of the numbers equals 0. When one of the numbers equals 0, the other number is the GCD. For example gcd⁡(5,0)=5\gcd(5,0) = 5 and gcd⁡(0,5)=5\gcd(0,5) = 5.

How do we know it will end?

In this process we are always subtracting the smaller number from the larger. Since both numbers are positive integers, each subtraction decreases their sum. This process cannot continue forever and eventually one of the numbers must become 0.

Next steps

Now for some longer question repeatedly subtracting can get tedious and so we divide and take remainders as shortcut.

For example, consider gcd⁡(40,4)\gcd(40,4). Instead of subtracting 4 from 40 ten times, we can simply divide, which is shown below. 40=10(4)+040 = 10(4) + 0 which gives us gcd⁡(4,0)\gcd(4,0) and thus GCD is 4.

Lets try 42,12

gcd⁡(42,12)\gcd(42,12)

Using our original method of we get 42−12−12−12=642−3(12)=642=3(12)+6\begin{aligned} 42 - 12 - 12 - 12 & = 6 \\ 42 - 3(12) & = 6 \\ 42 & = 3(12) + 6 \end{aligned}

Notice how we had to subtract 12 three times to get 6 as an answer. Instead of repeated arithmetic, we could simply divide 42 by 12 and take the remainder.

42=3(12)+642 = 3(12) + 6

Now we can rearrange the GCD problem as gcd⁡(12,6)\gcd(12,6) And now by looking we can guess the GCD must be 6, using the algorithm once again we obtain

12=2(6)+012 = 2(6) + 0

gcd⁡(12,6)=gcd⁡(6,0)=6\gcd(12,6) = \gcd(6,0) = 6

Division is not a new method, its is just repeated substraction, that makes our method faster and easier. Thus we no need to prove this seperately.

So Division is the next step of making this method even faster, by doing so we have reinvented the Euclidean algorithm.
